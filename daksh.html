Gated — A Smart Visitor Management System

Built for Christ University's DAKSH / Open Day (December 2–3, 2025), its annual education fair in Bengaluru — replacing a manual, Excel-and-Google-Forms visitor process with QR verification, volunteer tracking, and live analytics.

Context

Introducing prospective students to the university

DAKSH / Open Day is Christ University's annual educational fair in Bengaluru, introducing prospective Class 11/12 students and their families to the university's courses, campus, and educational offering — combining course inquiry with campus tours and substantial visitor footfall.

The problem

Lived firsthand, as a volunteer

During my first year participating, I worked as a volunteer and experienced the manual visitor-management process directly. Participants registered through Google Forms, arrived with email confirmations, and were checked against Excel lists by hand. Volunteers then coordinated visitor groups and tour assignments manually.

  • Entry verification required volunteers to manually locate and validate participant records.
  • Organizers had limited structured visibility into which volunteers took which groups, and how many tours they completed.
  • Event data lived in spreadsheets, making real-time footfall and course-interest analysis difficult.
  • Manual records left room for inconsistent reporting.

The problem was discovered through conversations with volunteers and organizers, combined with first-hand observation — not a formal research study.

Solution

Pitched to the university IT department as a finished idea

After experiencing the process as a volunteer, I co-developed Gated and pitched it to the university IT department for use at the event. The idea originated with us; IT reviewed, tested, and requested changes — as a stakeholder, not as the builder.

Registration & QR verification

Participants registered through a link shared in invitation emails and on event posters, capturing name, phone, email, and course of interest. Submission auto-generated a QR code tied to a unique participant ID. At the entrance, volunteers used scanning devices or phones — a scan retrieved registered details including course interest and accompanying-person information, flagged invalid registrations, and replaced manual Excel searching entirely.

Duplicate scans were prevented at the source: each QR code carried a unique ID, and the moment it was scanned, that ID was marked as checked in. A second scan of the same code was recognized immediately rather than creating a duplicate entry.

Volunteer allocation & tour tracking

Participants were assigned to an available volunteer (target group size: 15 participants per volunteer). Each volunteer was identified by university register number, letting the system associate participants with the responsible volunteer and retain tour activity records — timestamps captured automatically, so administrators could review tours completed per volunteer across the two-day event.

Dashboard & analytics

A real-time administrative dashboard showed participant numbers, course preferences, and volunteer activity — filterable and exportable, giving organizers a clearer view of total footfall and relative course interest than spreadsheets alone could. Charts were built using TypeScript-compatible data visualization libraries such as Recharts and Chart.js.

Live-event challenge

The same failure Socio hit — found independently

On day one, within approximately the first two hours of the live event, the application struggled to retrieve a specific participant record from a list of around 1,500 records. We traced it to loading the large list without pagination.

We implemented pagination at 50 records per page and deployed the fix that afternoon — while the event was still running. After the change, the application operated reliably for the rest of the event.

A practical lesson in designing and testing for production conditions, rather than assuming a system that works in development will perform equally well under live operational load. Participant-data quality and early connectivity issues were separate challenges handled the same way — on the day, under pressure.

Reported outcomes

Faster, and more visible to organizers

  • Faster entry: processing fell from an estimated 5–10 minutes in the previous manual workflow (based on my own volunteer experience) to under one minute, observed during the event.
  • Smoother peak flow: multiple volunteers scanning on available devices — no observed queues during busy periods.
  • Better traceability: participant-to-volunteer mappings and timestamped activity made review straightforward.
  • Accessible analytics: organizers viewed footfall and course-interest data through dashboards rather than spreadsheet analysis alone.
  • Stakeholder feedback: the IT department, university management, and the Pro Vice Chancellor gave verbal appreciation; participants and parents responded positively to the digital process.
  • Potential reuse: the university indicated the system would be used for a future DAKSH / Open Day — proposed, not yet confirmed as having happened.
What I'd improve next

Beyond a free-tier pilot

  • Move beyond a free-tier student deployment and test against larger datasets and higher traffic.
  • Design a controlled offline/fallback check-in process for connectivity failures.
  • Deepen course-demand and visitor-behaviour analytics.
  • Strengthen registration validation earlier, to reduce inconsistent records before event day.

The project demonstrated the real difference between building in a development environment and operating during a live event — data quality, connectivity, retrieval performance, and time-sensitive failures all show up only under real operational conditions.

Current status

Deployment and ownership

The application remains under the developers' deployment and has not yet been used for another event. The university has indicated intent to use it for a future DAKSH / Open Day; the deployment is currently associated with the developers rather than handed over to university IT.
