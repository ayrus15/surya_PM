01 — The problem

Fragmented across WhatsApp, email, and separate forms

As a student, I repeatedly ran into a simple but widespread problem: events and fests were happening across the university, but there was no centralized way to discover what was on that day, that week, or soon. Information was scattered across WhatsApp messages, email announcements, departmental communications, and separate Google Forms — discovering an event, registering, and accessing attendance information each required a disconnected process.

Conversations with university stakeholders revealed the problem went beyond student discovery: organizers and administrators also needed structured approval processes, visibility into event activity, and reliable participation data. The core problem was never the absence of an event-listing website — it was the lack of a unified system connecting the event lifecycle for students, organizers, and administration.

02 — Discovery & validation

Two user groups, different needs

We began with a problem I had personally experienced as a student, but didn't assume my own experience was sufficient validation. Together, we engaged more than 200 students through conversations and research to test whether fragmented event discovery was a broader problem, then worked with university leadership, the IT department, technology providers, and the incubation ecosystem to understand institutional needs and constraints.

Students needed a centralized place to discover events, register easily, and access tickets. University administration needed controlled approvals, organizational oversight, reliable participation records, and analytics at different administrative levels. A significant institutional concern was third-party management of student data — this shaped later decisions on authentication and hosting.

Key insight: a successful product had to satisfy both the student experience and the university's operational requirements — not one at the expense of the other.

03 — Product evolution

Two pivots, each forced by evidence

1

Original vision — student networking

A campus-focused platform where students could connect, collaborate, and work on ideas and projects together.

2

MVP — event creation & registration

Narrowed to a single concrete workflow: organizers publish events, students register.

3

Pivot — institutional event management

Expanded to include approval workflows, QR ticketing, attendance, real-time visibility, and administrative analytics — driven by what institutional stakeholders actually needed.

Scope discipline: direct student messaging was deliberately excluded because of potential misuse and the moderation/safety requirements it would have added — a feature cut for governance reasons, not laziness.

04 — Solution & core workflows

From discovery to institutional reporting

Multi-stage approval workflow

Events follow a defined approval path involving the event coordinator, Head of Department, Dean, and university management, including the CFO where required — making publication dependent on required approvals rather than informal coordination.

05 — Data governance & institutional trust

Treating governance as a product requirement

Because Socio operates with student identities and event records, data management was an important consideration during institutional discussions. Proposed approaches included a formal data-use agreement and university-email authentication. The team proceeded with email-based login/verification to restrict access to university users and avoid manually requesting profile information already available through university identity.

The current deployment uses external cloud/company-controlled infrastructure; the application stores identity details, registrations, attendance, and other event records. University-controlled hosting was considered as an alternative.

06 — Reported outcomes

What shipped, and what wasn't measured

The project produced a centralized event discovery and registration experience, a structured approval workflow, QR-based ticketing and attendance, and administrative dashboards and analytics.

Stated plainly, not glossed over: percentage improvements in participation, efficiency, or time saved are not claimed, because those outcomes were not quantified in this pilot.

I co-built Socio with a small founding team, and this case study focuses on the parts I worked on directly.

07 — Reflection & roadmap

What I'd measure next

  • Measure outcomes, not just usage: registration-to-attendance conversion, repeat participation, and organizer satisfaction would better capture sustained value.
  • Treat governance as a product requirement: institutional workflows, permissions, and data handling belong alongside the UI and core features, not after them.
  • Design for scale early: performance and retrieval need testing against realistic registration volumes before launch, not discovery under load.

Socio taught me that product development isn't simply converting an idea into software — it's identifying the right problem, validating it with users, understanding organizational constraints, prioritizing competing needs, and executing a solution that works in practice.
